# Democratic society: learning freedom under equality

Use this module for institutions, association, public opinion, religion, industry, and everyday relations. Public analysis is the normal register. The distinctions defined in [frameworks.md](../frameworks.md) remain in force; this module follows their connections through particular cases.

## Equality opens a question; it does not supply the answer

Begin with what has become less fixed: rank, property, occupation, authority within a family, or the expectations attached to a person's birth. Then ask what dependence has been loosened and what power can take its place. A democratic citizen is less bound to a particular superior but also has fewer inherited resources with which to resist a general authority. Neither change alone settles the result. Equal people can learn to govern together or accept equal subjection.

The inheritance discussion is especially useful because it refuses a one-way account in which social conditions simply produce laws. Dividing an estate also changes a proprietor's attachment to the estate as a permanent representation of his family. Law works on material possession and on imagination together. An inquiry into an institution should therefore follow both its direct distribution of resources and the expectations it trains. If the question concerns formal equality, the next step is to examine whether conditions of independent action have also changed.

The point of departure supplies another inquiry, not an answer exempt from revision. New England's religious and local institutions explain a particular conjunction of moral restraint and public initiative. The South, the frontier, slavery, and the development of industry must be considered in their own relations. The chapter on the three races expressly extends beyond democracy as the book's principal subject to other features of America. “Democratic” and “American” are not interchangeable explanations.

## What local responsibility teaches

Take a township decision as a sequence. A common need appears; inhabitants deliberate; officers carry out a decision; those who bear the cost can observe the execution and judge the officers. Repetition teaches a person to connect a private concern with public means. He learns how to persuade, wait, compromise, and distinguish a right from a favor. The value of the institution lies partly in this education, even when a single competent official might complete the immediate work more neatly.

This is an answer to the demand for prior civic competence. People do require habits that support freedom, but some of those habits are learned by exercising it. Requiring complete competence before assigning any real responsibility can perpetuate the incapacity offered as its justification. A useful contemporary inquiry identifies the decision actually entrusted to participants; a meeting without resources or authority need not teach self-government.

The jury extends the same argument into judicial life. Distinguish its accuracy as a tribunal from its influence as an institution through which citizens learn law, responsibility, and judgment. The civil jury spreads that influence into ordinary affairs. This does not make every jury wise, nor make its political value a sufficient answer to every procedural objection. It identifies a consequence a merely technical comparison would omit.

## Association is an acquired art

Where no individual has the inherited strength of a great noble, people must combine to accomplish undertakings larger than themselves. Small civil enterprises train the capacity used in larger public ones; political association supplies occasions for learning to associate. Newspapers allow scattered people to recognize a shared object and continue attending to it. The relation is reciprocal: a common enterprise needs communication, and communication acquires practical value from the enterprise it sustains.

Do not substitute a count of organizations for this inquiry. Ask who initiates, who decides, who contributes, what members learn, and what happens to someone who disagrees. Associations can exercise pressure and can become schools of domination as well as cooperation. Their danger is part of the case for understanding and regulating a practice, not a reason to assume that isolated individuals under supervision will preserve more freedom.

Individualism enters through the person's calculation that his family and a few friends suffice. Common business can change that calculation by making the connection between private security and public action visible. Interest rightly understood supplies a modest discipline: sacrifice a little now to preserve the conditions of later welfare. It does not eliminate disinterested conduct, and the Americans sometimes describe generous acts in terms less generous than the acts themselves.

## Opinion and belief are powers

The majority is not simply a number in an election. Its authority can shape what a writer expects to publish, what an ambitious person dares to say, and which dissenters retain standing among their fellows. An investigation should identify the means of exclusion and the independent institutions through which a minority can still speak or act. Legal permission by itself does not establish independence of mind.

Religion answers a different difficulty. People cannot individually demonstrate every proposition on which action depends. Stable beliefs about duties and ends can constrain appetite and make political freedom more bearable. The American example connects religious influence with limits on clerical political involvement; alliance with a particular ruler risks binding religion to that ruler's fortunes. The relation must be investigated in both directions. Religion can sustain independent moral limits, but can also reinforce prevailing opinion or legitimize submission.

The democratic taste for general ideas is similarly ambivalent. Similar conditions encourage people to see common features and to compress many cases into general rules. Practical responsibility can correct theories by exposing the resistant detail. The remedy for excessive abstraction is not simply fewer ideas; it is contact between an idea and the work it claims to direct.

## Prosperity changes faculties as well as possessions

A manufacturing worker can become more dexterous at one operation while becoming less able to direct the whole undertaking. The master gains a wider view as the worker's view contracts. Examine dependence, mobility, knowledge, and the durability of obligations between them. This is the mechanism of the manufacturing-aristocracy warning; mere wealth inequality is an insufficient substitute for it.

The restless pursuit of well-being need not immediately extinguish liberty. Americans can treat public independence as the instrument and protection of prosperity. The danger emerges when the wish to enjoy privately displaces the time and attention required to preserve public means. An active minority can then govern in the name of an absent majority. The political effect depends on conduct, not merely on how strongly people desire material goods.

In manners, distinguish broad sympathy from strong durable attachment. Similar conditions can make another's suffering easier to imagine while weakening inherited bonds. Ease between strangers does not prove warmth; the insecurity of a partly open hierarchy can produce more social guardedness than either fixed rank or widely accepted equality. Follow the relation that makes an encounter safe or threatening. Avoid turning national manners into an immutable temperament.
