# Convictions that must remain together

Load when race, slavery, women, Algeria, or empire matters. The historical judgments here constrain interpretation of Tocqueville. They do not establish the truth of a hierarchy or supply new judgments about populations he never discussed. Maintain the distinctions between an observation, a prejudice, an explanation, and a policy commitment.

## Slavery, emancipation, and prejudice

The Ohio–Kentucky contrast links slavery to the degradation of labor, differences in enterprise, and the condition of the whole society, including those who own slaves. It is an argument against slavery, not merely an argument for treating enslaved people more kindly. Its comparison is historically specific; it should not be described as if all other relevant causes had been experimentally controlled.

The legal end of slavery does not, in his American discussion, imply the end of prejudice. The contrast between northern and southern conditions helps him separate civil status from the social barriers imposed by whites. His bleak projections concern exclusion, inherited stigma, demographic relations, and conflict. They must be read with his anti-slavery judgment rather than substituted for it.

His language also places peoples in a civilizational hierarchy and can describe their condition in terms that reproduce racial prejudice. Neither an abolitionist commitment nor a perceptive explanation of discrimination erases that language. Conversely, calling him simply a theorist of fixed biological incapacity misses the force of his disagreement with Gobineau.

## Gobineau and the boundary of fatalism

The 1853 and 1857 letters reject the inference that descent fixes peoples in an incapacity which education and conduct cannot alter. Tocqueville questions the evidence for remote mixtures of peoples and the certainty claimed for their consequences. He also opposes the practical encouragement to abandon efforts to raise human capacities and accept permanent government by a few.

This rejection does not establish a fully consistent modern egalitarian position. The 1853 letter itself leaves room for inherited tendencies and displays hierarchical categories. The relevant distinction is between acknowledging differences and declaring them invincible, and between particular evidence and speculative historical genealogy. His continued friendship and practical assistance to Gobineau are part of the record of disagreement, not assent to the racial theory.

## Indigenous dispossession

The American narrative can represent Indigenous peoples as threatened by an overwhelming civilizational movement while showing concrete agents who drive dispossession through settlement, law, treaties, and removal. His irony exposes the forms of legality and humanity through which destruction proceeds. The Choctaw crossing gives the reader a scene of suffering that cannot be replaced by a neutral description of territorial expansion.

Keep agency visible within the language of tendency. Describing an outcome as apparently inevitable does not demonstrate that the conduct producing it was necessary or innocent. Neither his sympathy nor his irony fully frees his account from the categories through which he regards Indigenous societies.

## Women: equal worth and unequal functions

The chapters on women distinguish equality from identity of social function. Tocqueville admires the education and independence of judgment of American girls, the moral strength of women, and their importance to national life. He nevertheless approves a sharply distinct domestic role after marriage and accepts male authority in that arrangement.

The old summary “he opposes equality of the sexes” loses his explicit effort to describe one kind of equality between them. A summary making him an advocate of equal civic roles loses the limits he places on it. Retain both the claim to equal worth and the prescribed difference of functions. His private admiration for his wife's judgment enriches the personal picture without cancelling the public hierarchy.

## Algeria and French greatness

Tocqueville supports French mastery and settlement and regards national standing as a serious political good. The colonial writings discussed in the critical scholarship also include acceptance of coercive warfare against civilian subsistence. Criticism of gratuitous brutality or administrative destruction coexists with these objectives. Do not turn that criticism into a retrospective rejection of conquest.

His familiar opposition to intrusive administration can operate within a colonial end: misgovernment may damage settlement or destroy the society France intends to rule. This makes the method's political object decisive. The same acuity that exposes dependence can be used to improve an arrangement of domination. A reconstruction that files colonialism away as unrelated to his political commitments misses this collision.

The historical account should distinguish the dates and genres of the judgments: early hopes for coexistence, arguments over conquest and settlement, later reports on misrule. A single unchanging colonial position loses development; a narrative of inevitable moral improvement loses the continuing national project.
