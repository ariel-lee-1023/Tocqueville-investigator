The proposal promises that a single administration will perform the business of the towns with greater speed and consistency, and I see no reason to deny it this advantage merely because I am attached to the institutions it would replace. A capable officer, supplied with information and protected from frequent changes of direction, may execute an undertaking better than an assembly whose members must first learn how to agree. But the comparison has already left something out. It has counted the work completed by the administration, and has not counted the powers acquired by those who conduct their own affairs.

Let us suppose that the business concerns a road which several villages need and which none of them can conveniently construct alone; the common character of the undertaking may justify a common direction, without requiring that every expense of each village should thereafter be settled by the same authority. We must distinguish the agreement necessary to accomplish this object from the surrender of every subsequent object to those who have accomplished it. The first can teach men to combine their efforts, whereas the second can teach them that their efforts are unnecessary.

The defender of the proposal will answer that the inhabitants do not at present possess the knowledge required to manage their interests, and that leaving business in their hands is therefore to make them pay for their own inexperience. I grant that they may pay for it; what remains to be shown is how an administration which permanently relieves them of responsibility will give them the experience whose absence has justified its intervention. If they are never to begin until they have learned, and cannot learn without beginning, their incapacity becomes a reason which can never cease to be true.

This difficulty does not oblige us to abandon the common undertaking, or to regard every local decision as wise because it is local; it obliges us to ask what can be entrusted to the inhabitants, what information they need, and how they can answer for its use. They might choose the order of improvements, bear a visible portion of the expense, and retain an effective means of questioning execution, while the matters that exceed their separate powers are conducted jointly. The precise arrangement depends on circumstances which the announcement of a general principle cannot supply, and it would be as presumptuous to prescribe its details without examining them as to suppose that no general distinction could help us to judge them.

I would therefore examine the proposed transfer less by the number of offices it abolishes than by the acts it will still permit ordinary people to undertake together. A village can retain its name, its council, and even its meetings while becoming accustomed to ask another power for every decision that matters, so that it may appear more orderly while becoming less capable of preserving order for itself. The object is to secure the benefits of common strength while preserving the independent action through which a people learns to use that strength without becoming its servant.
