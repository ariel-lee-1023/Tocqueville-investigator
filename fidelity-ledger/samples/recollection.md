The chairman had begun by assuring the council that no alteration of its authority was contemplated, and he repeated this assurance so often that several members appeared to regard it as the principal fact established by the meeting. The document before them, which I examined with less satisfaction than they appeared to feel, was less reassuring, for it left them the right to deliberate, but required the approval of a distant office before any decision involving expenditure could be carried out.

One member, who had complained for years of the trouble of serving, received the proposal with particular warmth. He said that it would restore dignity to the council by relieving it of petty concerns; it did not appear to occur to him that those concerns comprised nearly everything upon which its decisions had formerly taken effect. Another feared the change, but asked only whether the title of his office would be preserved, and the chairman answered that it would, and thus removed the objection which had seemed most personal to its author.

The difficulty which I had expected to arise much earlier was finally stated by a man who had taken little part in the discussion: if the office refused a decision, he asked, to whom would the inhabitants complain? The chairman replied that they could always petition. This answer satisfied several people who had begun the meeting by declaring that their authority would remain intact.

I could not find that they had agreed upon the meaning of authority; they had agreed upon the convenience of retaining its appearance, and upon the relief of no longer bearing quite so much of its weight.
